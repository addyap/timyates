Assurance vie explained: why it suits so many people living in France

By Tim Yates · 6 min read

If you've moved to France you'll hear about assurance vie within weeks. Your neighbours have one, your bank wants to sell you one, and your notaire will mention it the moment inheritance comes up. The name puts many British people off, because it sounds like life cover. It isn't. It's a savings and investment wrapper, and for most French residents it's the first place I look.

What it is

An assurance vie is a contract with an insurance company. You pay money in, as a lump sum or regularly, and it's invested in funds you choose: anything from cautious euro funds to shares and bonds. You can usually take money out whenever you like. There's no lock-in and no upper limit.

The French state treats it generously, because it wants people to save. That's where the advantages come from.

How it's taxed while you're alive

While your money stays inside the contract, growth isn't taxed each year. You only pay tax when you take money out, and only on the part of each withdrawal that is gain, not on the capital you put in.

The real benefit comes after eight years. From the eighth anniversary of the contract, you get an annual allowance on the gains you withdraw: currently €4,600 for a single person and €9,200 for a couple. Gains above that are taxed at a reduced flat rate. French social charges still apply to the gains, but because only a slice of each withdrawal is gain, the effective tax on your income can be very low.

The eight-year clock starts when the contract opens, not when you add money. Opening one early, even with a small amount, is often worth it.

Why it matters for inheritance

French inheritance tax can be heavy, especially for stepchildren and people who aren't related to you. Assurance vie sits largely outside the normal rules.

  • You can name whoever you like as beneficiaries.
  • For money paid in before you're 70, each beneficiary can currently receive up to €152,500 free of French inheritance tax, on top of their normal allowances.
  • For money paid in after 70, there is a smaller shared allowance on the premiums, but all the growth passes free of inheritance tax.
  • A spouse or PACS partner is exempt from French inheritance tax anyway.

For blended families in particular, this can make an enormous difference.

French or international?

You can buy assurance vie from a French bank, but you don't have to. Contracts from insurers in Luxembourg and Ireland are built to French tax rules and often give you a wider choice of funds, and the option to hold some of your money in sterling or other currencies. For people with income or family in the UK, that flexibility can be valuable. Luxembourg contracts also come with strong protections for policyholders' money.

Is it right for you?

It depends on your plans, your family, how long you expect to stay in France and what you already hold. For many people it's the cornerstone of their savings here. For others, part of their money is better elsewhere. That's the conversation I'd want to have with you.
