Buying a home in France with a mortgage: how it really works

By Tim Yates · 7 min read

French mortgages can be very good value, and you don't need to live in France to get one. But the rules are tighter than many British buyers expect, and the timetable is fixed by law once you sign. The people who struggle are usually the ones who found the house first and the finance second.

The rules lenders work to

French banks follow guidance from the country's financial stability regulator. In practice that means:

  • Affordability is capped. Your total monthly loan repayments, including the new mortgage and its insurance, generally can't exceed 35% of your income.
  • Terms are generally limited to 25 years, with a little longer allowed for some new builds.
  • Deposits matter. Buyers who aren't resident in France are often asked for a larger deposit than residents, and lenders like to see savings left over after the purchase.
  • Income in another currency is accepted by many lenders, though some count less of it to allow for exchange-rate swings.

Budget for the costs on top

The purchase costs often called "notaire's fees" are mostly taxes. For an older property they're typically around 7 to 8% of the price; for a new build, much less. Lenders usually expect you to pay these from your own money, so plan for them on top of your deposit.

Mortgage insurance

French lenders will expect your loan to be covered by borrower insurance (assurance emprunteur) against death and, often, disability. You don't have to take the bank's own policy, and an external one can cost considerably less. For smaller loans that end before your 60th birthday, there's no longer a medical questionnaire; otherwise, health matters, and it's worth knowing early whether yours will need special treatment.

The timetable

  1. Before you offer: get an idea of what you can borrow. It strengthens your position with the seller and avoids heartbreak.
  2. The compromis de vente: the preliminary contract. You'll usually pay a deposit to the notaire. You then have a 10-day cooling-off period.
  3. The finance condition: the compromis should include a clause that lets you withdraw and recover your deposit if you can't get a mortgage. The period is set in the contract, often 45 to 60 days. This is where speed matters.
  4. The loan offer: by law you can't accept it until 10 full days after you receive it. Build that into your timetable.
  5. Completion at the notaire's office, typically two to three months after the compromis.
